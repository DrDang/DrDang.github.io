## The Columbus expedition made our modern world
This topic was by far my biggest take away. Some interesting points follow:
- The 'world' is a complex system outside of directed control. A fascinating example of **emergence** cited in the book is the 'Little Ice Age.' Mann suggests this may have been caused by the rapid decline of Native American populations due to European disease. This cessation of human-driven forest burning led to massive tree growth, carbon sequestration, and essentially cooled the planet.
- The real "manifest destiny", if there were such a thing, appears to be globalization. I originally believed it to be a recent phenomena made possible by technology such as large cargo ships, telephony, and the internet when in actuality it was already realized after Columbus connected the world through the Columbian exchange and simply amplified by modern technology. Regardless of trajectory, the endpoint was the globalization that we're experiencing today.
- Another emergent property of the globalization resulting from the Columbian exchange is the "Homogenocene", the phenomenon where our world is becoming more "same" everywhere due to human driven globalization. Potatoes, tomatoes, and corn were once unique to the Americas but now are prevalent world wide and even form classical foods of many cultures outside the Americas.
- Our modern world of spaghetti and red sauce Italian food, European potato cuisine, rubber plants being in South Asia was thanks to Columbus. Historical food staples of many cultures in Europe, China, and the Phillipines were really only "historical" as far back at post 1493!

## On Slavery, Ethnicity, and Race
- Race, as how it's perceived in recent times and especially culturally, is a more modern phenomena. Mann argues that initially, the African slave trade was driven less by racial ideology and more by biological resistance to malaria and the ready supply of slaves resulting from the African slave trade. These circumstances made African labor an economically logical choice before it mutated into the chattel form of slavery that we know today. This resulted in some other interesting phenomena such as Africans being the majority people in the Americas for a period of time when most people going to Americas were the slave labor force for Tobacco and sugar plantations.
- Indentured servants from Europe became disfavored for slaves because indentured servants often took land and became competition to the already established plantations after their contracts ended. They were also more prone to death due to the harsh conditions of the Americas.
- Native American Indian slavery, already present in the new world, intensified due to their demand for flintlock guns from the West. The colonies needed labor and the tribes needed flintlock guns. This created a **reinforcing feedback loop**: tribes needed guns to survive, so they captured slaves to trade for guns, which required them to capture more slaves. This loop also resulted in competing colonies ally with competing tribes since the losing tribe would become slave labor for the "winning colony", and likewise the winning tribe would then get flintlocks, further reinforcing this loop.
- There was a complex relationship between black slave owners holding Native American Indians as slaves and Native American Indian slave owners holding black slaves beyond the traditionally well understood white slave owner dynamic.
- In South America, escaped slaves resulted in new colonies resulting from mixing of African and the Native population, which would be called "Maroons".
- Also interesting was that slavery was not seen as morally abhorrent back then in the same sense it is viewed today and in fact there were even a whole different set of ethics applied to slavery (e.g. Christians could not enslave other Christians, Muslims could not enslave fellow Muslims, etc.)
- People were typically more identified via their religion back then (e.g., Christians, Muslims) rather than the geography from where they came (e.g. Genoa, Spain)

## The Galleon Trade
- Trade was tough with China due to there simply being no demand for Western goods in China to begin with; they were satisfied with what they had until Western silver became available
- China’s monetary instability (where new dynasties would render previous currency worthless) created an intense demand for silver as a stable store of value. The Americas had the supply, Spain controlled the logistics, and the Philippines served as the trade hub.
- Eventually this made silver the mandatory currency for tax payments 

## Early American Colonial Life
- That early life in the Americas was so dreadful due to mosquitos, malaria, swarms of ants, hostile native tribes, and difficulty in growing food. Large numbers of deaths were common.

# Conclusion
Columbus changed the world more than just "discovering the Americas". He essentially closed the last gap required to fully close the link to globalization, starting a series of world defining events such as the tobacco and sugar frenzy, slavery in the Americas, transportation of wildlife and plants between the continents, the resulting evolution of those plants (such as the tomato), the silver trade with China and economic impacts resulting thereof, and of course the rapid colonization of the Americas. The modern world is an emergent property of these complex, often brutal, historical exchanges. While we cannot change the suffering that fueled this globalization, _1493_ provides a framework to understand how deeply connected our economic and ecological systems truly are. Capitalism continues to fuel modern globalism with supply and demand continuing to cut across borders.
