When I was in middle school, my obsession with anime led me to learn Japanese, as most shows were in Japanese with English subtitles. Because Japanese is often deemed one of the hardest languages for native English speakers to learn (and I wanted to understand it as fast as possible), I began researching the most effective methods for language acquisition. My research pointed to two major findings:

1. **Consistent Immersion** (e.g., listening, speaking, and reading regularly).
2. **SRS (Spaced Repetition System) Flash Cards.**

One surprise from this adventure was that it didn't just teach me Japanese; it taught me _how to learn_ across any domain. I have used these exact techniques to fast-track my preparation for the FE, CCNA, PE, and INCOSE SEP exams. Most recently, it allowed me to spin up at a new job quickly enough to lead a successful Critical Design Review (CDR) on a project I had only been involved with for a few months, enough so to win an award for excellence!

I want to share what I’ve learned about learning, and how these techniques can help you.

## Immersion

The best way to learn Japanese is to live in Japan. Why? Because you are forced to read, speak, and hear the language every day. It takes discipline to recreate this immersion if you aren't physically there, but today’s technology makes it easier than ever.

You can apply this same "digital immersion" to any domain. In my engineering career, this means exposure beyond the 9-to-5. I don't just learn about RF systems at work; I read Wikipedia articles, watch YouTube deep-dives, and debate topics with AI models to test my understanding. The goal is to surround yourself with the topic constantly, creating a feedback loop that feeds into the SRS Flash Cards.

## SRS Flash Cards

This is the "secret weapon." SRS, or Spaced Repetition System, is an approach to flashcards that optimizes your memory. The algorithm forces you to review content you struggle with frequently, while pushing content you know well further into the future, ideally showing it to you right before you are about to forget it.

This planned repetition moves information from short-term to long-term memory efficiently. While many tools exist, the industry standard (and my personal favorite) is **Anki**.

The process is simple:

1. The app shows you a card (which you create).
2. You mentally guess the answer.
3. You flip the card and rate how well you knew it (e.g., Did you forget entirely? Was it a struggle? Was it easy?).
4. The algorithm schedules the next review based on your rating.

I do these flashcards every day, adding new cards whenever I encounter a new concept.

### Example Workflow

#### Doing Flashcards

Every day, whenever I have free time whether its sitting on the couch, walking outside, standing in line, I pull out the Anki app on my phone. It can be mentally draining, so I often gamify it. For example, if I burn my "due cards" down to a certain number, I reward myself with 5 minutes of scrolling X (Twitter). I do this in bursts until all cards due for the day are finished.

#### Creating Flashcards

My deep study sessions involve reading articles or books and creating flashcards immediately when I hit a concept I need to retain—a new acronym, a definition, or a complex system interaction.

Recently, I have optimized this by using LLMs (like ChatGPT, Grok, or Gemini). I will discuss a topic with the AI, then ask it to generate copy-pasteable flashcards based on our conversation. I paste these directly into Anki. This helps solidify the questions and debates I’ve just had.

### Why I use Anki

I stick with Anki for one main reason: **Cloud Synchronization.** I can review cards on my phone while out and about, sync to the server, and pick up exactly where I left off on my desktop. This seamless transition removes friction and enables the consistency required for the method to work.

# Conclusion

This technique has drastically improved my life, not just for languages, but for mastering the technical requirements of my career quickly and permanently. The results speak for themselves, and I hope these techniques help you in your own continuous learning journey. Godspeed!
