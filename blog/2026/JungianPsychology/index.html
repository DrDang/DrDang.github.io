Folk Theory and Horoscopes - A Review of Jungian Psychology Step by Step

Created in January 01, 2026

2026   ·   Psychology   ·   Psychology

The book Jungian Psychology Step by Step aims to introduce readers to Jung, but I found it poorly written…the literary equivalent of rambling. Much of the writing reminded me less of intellectual discourse and more of horoscopes.

Format aside, Jungian psychology itself felt to me more like folk theory, mysticism, and philosophy than actual science. Below are my thoughts on the introductory concepts that stood out.

The Collective Unconscious

The idea of a transcendent psychology from which common themes and archetypes are independently derived, the Collective Unconscious, is interesting, but ultimately strikes me as folk theory rather than an observable psychological phenomenon.

Archetypes

Stemming from the idea of the Collective Unconscious is the concept of “Archetypes.” These are common narrative characters Jung observed manifesting across cultures via their stories. Examples include the Hero (who undergoes the hero’s journey), the Wise Old Man/Woman (the “guide” character), and the Trickster (who causes chaos), amongst others.

The Shadow

The Shadow describes the aspects of our unconscious that we keep hidden, yet continue to follow us (like a shadow). Jung suggested “shadow work,” a process where individuals learn to recognize these dark traits on their journey toward “wholeness” or “individuation.” The goal is not to continue suppressing the shadow, but rather to recognize it and become conscious of it to improve one’s psychology.

Symbols and Myths

A recurring theme with Jung is the importance of symbols, myths, and religion. He argued that they represent the human psyche and studied them accordingly.

Synchronicity

Synchronicity is the concept of “meaningful coincidental encounters.” A famous anecdote from Jung himself involves a patient he described as being particularly rational. She was describing a dream where she was given a golden scarab; at that exact moment, a real, rare, scarab-like beetle flew into Jung’s office.

The event was so unlikely that it supposedly dissolved her rational barrier, allowing therapy to finally progress. Jung argued that such coincidences were connected by meaning rather than cause and effect. This idea is rightfully criticized as it implies metaphysical forces are at play. The the most charitable interpretation is that there are no metaphysics involved, but rather that coincidences become meaningful simply because they reveal something internal about us. Personally, I find this concept to be at high risk of confirmation bias.

Conclusion

Jungian Psychology seems to be a bridge between philosophy and the science of psychology, relying heavily on folk theory. While this makes his ideas less credible as hard science, they represent an interesting approach to understanding the mind, anchored deeply in the religious culture of his time.
