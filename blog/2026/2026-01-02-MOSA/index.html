MOSA stands for “Modular Open Systems Approach.” It is an acquisition and technical strategy defined in a recent GAO report as “…a combination of engineering and business practices in which weapons systems are designed with modular components that are linked by clearly defined system interfaces.”

Although not a new concept (evidence shows DoD interest going as far back as 1994 with the Open Systems Joint Task Force) there is yet to even be a specific Wikipedia article for it! Despite the lack of Wiki-fame, it is required by law. As of the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for Fiscal Year 2017, programs are statutorily required to implement MOSA wherever practical.

Yet, a 2023 GAO report indicates implementation remains weak across the DoD portfolio. This post breaks down the two key features of MOSA, Modularity and Open Standard Interfaces, and how to identify the “MOSA Boundary” in real-world systems.

1. Modularity

To be modular means a system is defined by functional units that can be replaced with equivalent units as needed (e.g., during maintenance or upgrades).

The GAO provides a relatable analogy: The Kitchen. Appliances, such as a refrigerator, can be replaced or upgraded with a unit from any vendor. This is enabled because of:

  1. Standard Sizing: The fridge fits in the hole.

  2. Standard Interfaces: The 120V power outlet is universal.

MOSA seeks to apply these same benefits to the DoD weapons portfolio to break vendor lock and speed up technology insertion.

2. Open Standard Interfaces

The “Open Standards” requirement often raises the question: Where in the system must the interface be open?

The cleanest answer depends on your definition of the Line Replaceable Unit (LRU). As the name implies, an LRU is a component that can be replaced “on the line” (a term derived from the aerospace “flight line” where aircraft are parked/maintained).

  • Inside the LRU: Interconnections are of no concern to the integrator and can remain proprietary/closed.

  • Outside the LRU: Interfaces connecting the LRU to the platform must be Open.

Thus, defining the LRU enables discovery of the MOSA boundary.

Concrete Example: The Cockpit Display

Consider a basic display for an aircraft cockpit. It has two connectors on the back: Power and Video Input.

In this scenario:

  • The LRU: The entire display unit. If it breaks, the maintenance crew swaps the whole box.

  • The MOSA Boundary: The Power and Data connectors on the back panel.

  • Not MOSA: The ribbon cables inside the box connecting the LCD panel to the internal circuit boards.

A Systems Engineer can use System Boundary Diagrams to formally document which interfaces cross the LRU threshold and therefore require open standards.

Case Study: Proprietary Implementation of Open Standards

Imagine a scenario where an LRU uses a MIL-DTL-38999 compliant physical connector. The pins are assigned standard roles: DC power, RJ-45 Ethernet, and I2C. These are all openly published standards.

Is this connector MOSA compliant? The answer is: Maybe.

The complication is the Pin Mapping (the specific arrangement of signals). To determine compliance, we apply the Litmus Test:

The MOSA Litmus Test: If the government were to replace this LRU with one from a competitor—changing nothing else (like the cabling harness)—would it simply work?

  • Scenario A (Compliant): The original vendor provided the pin mapping with Government Purpose Rights (GPR) or Unlimited Rights. Competitors can see exactly which pin does what, allowing them to build a compatible drop-in replacement.

  • Scenario B (Non-Compliant): The physical connector is standard, but the specific pinout is proprietary to the original vendor. The government is now vendor-locked. Even though they use a “standard” connector, no one else can build a box that fits the existing cable harness without reverse engineering or paying royalties.

This is a critical edge case. On the surface, the hardware is “Open,” but the data rights regarding the implementation (the pinout) disqualify it. The priority solution is to comply with defined pinout standards (e.g., SOSA, GVA, VICTORY) or publish the Interface Control Document (ICD) with unlimited rights.
